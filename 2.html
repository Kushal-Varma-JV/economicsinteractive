Micro Ch 4 · Theory of the Firm under Perfect Competition

Explain the determinants of a firm's supply curve.4 marks

💡 Remember: the supply curve is part of the MC curve, so whatever moves MC moves supply. Cheaper to produce → supply shifts right. Costlier to produce → supply shifts left.

Exam answer

A firm's supply curve is the rising part of its marginal cost (MC) curve, from the minimum point of AVC upwards. So anything that changes the MC curve also shifts the supply curve. There are two such factors:

a) Technological progress: with new technology or a better way of organising work, the same labour and capital produce more output. So the firm needs fewer inputs for each unit, and its MC falls (the MC curve shifts down, to the right). The supply curve shifts to the right: at every price, the firm supplies more.

b) Input prices: if the price of an input rises (for example, the wage rate of labour), the cost of production rises. Average cost and MC both rise (the MC curve shifts up, to the left). The supply curve shifts to the left: at every price, the firm supplies less. If input prices fall, the supply curve shifts right.

Extra point, unit tax: a tax of Rs t on each unit sold raises AC and MC by Rs t at every output, so it also shifts the supply curve to the left.

Write about Shut down point, Normal profit and Break-even point.4 marks

💡 Remember: Shut down point = lowest point of AVC. Break-even point = lowest point of AC. Normal profit = just enough profit to stay in business.

Exam answer

1. Shut down point: in the short run, a firm keeps producing as long as the price is at least equal to the minimum AVC. So the lowest point of the AVC curve, where the SMC curve cuts it, is the last price–output combination at which the firm produces. Below this price, it produces nothing. This is the short run shut down point. In the long run, the shut down point is the minimum point of the LRAC curve.

2. Normal profit: the minimum profit a firm needs to stay in its existing business. A firm that does not earn it will leave the business. So normal profit is counted as part of the firm's total cost. It can be thought of as the opportunity cost of entrepreneurship. Any profit above normal profit is called super-normal profit.

3. Break-even point: the point on the supply curve where the firm earns only normal profit (no super-normal profit and no loss). It is the minimum point of the average cost curve, where the supply (MC) curve cuts it: the SAC curve in the short run, and the LRAC curve in the long run. Here price = AC, so TR = TC.

What is price ceiling? Explain with a diagram.4 marks

💡 Remember: ceiling = the top of a room, so it is a maximum price. It is set below the equilibrium price, which causes a shortage (excess demand). That leads to rationing, queues and black markets.

Exam answer

A price ceiling is the upper limit (maximum price) fixed by the government on the price of a good or service. It is fixed below the market equilibrium price, because at the market price some poor people cannot afford these goods. It is generally imposed on essential goods like wheat, rice, kerosene and sugar. Example: the Public Distribution System (ration shops).

  • At the ceiling price Pc, consumers want to buy qc, but firms supply only q'c. So there is an excess demand (shortage) of q'c qc.
  • The limited supply is shared out through rationing: ration coupons are issued, and the good is sold through ration shops (fair price shops).
  • Bad effects: (a) consumers have to stand in long queues at ration shops; (b) people who do not get enough are willing to pay more, which can create a black market.

What is price floor? Explain with a diagram.4 marks

💡 Remember: floor = the bottom of a room, so it is a minimum price. It is set above the equilibrium price, which causes a surplus (excess supply). Examples: MSP for farmers, minimum wages.

Exam answer

A price floor is the lower limit (minimum price) fixed by the government on the price of a good or service. It is fixed above the market equilibrium price, because for some goods a very low price is not desirable.

  • Examples: (a) agricultural price support, such as the Minimum Support Price (MSP) for farmers; (b) minimum wage legislation (the Minimum Wages Act), which keeps the wage rate above the equilibrium wage.
  • At the floor price pf, consumers buy only qf, but firms want to sell q'f. So there is an excess supply (surplus) of qf q'f.
  • Under agricultural price support, the government must buy the surplus at the fixed price, to stop the price from falling.

Present the derivation of the slope of the Budget line.4 marks

💡 Remember: take two points on the budget line, subtract the two equations, and rearrange. Slope = −p1/p2. It is negative because more bananas means fewer mangoes.

Exam answer

The slope of the budget line is the change in mangoes (x2) needed for each unit change in bananas (x1) along the budget line. Take any two points on the budget line: (x1, x2) and (x1 + Δx1, x2 + Δx2). Both cost exactly the income M.

  1. First point: p1x1 + p2x2 = M …… (2.4)
  2. Second point: p1(x1 + Δx1) + p2(x2 + Δx2) = M …… (2.5)
  3. Subtract (2.4) from (2.5): p1Δx1 + p2Δx2 = 0 …… (2.6)
  4. Rearrange: p2Δx2 = −p1Δx1, so Δx2 ÷ Δx1 = −p1 ÷ p2 …… (2.7)

Meaning: to buy one more banana, the consumer must give up p1/p2 mangoes. The absolute slope p1/p2 is the rate at which the market lets her swap bananas for mangoes when she spends all her income.

Explain the Indifference Map with a diagram.4 marks

💡 Remember: indifference map = a family of indifference curves. The higher the curve, the happier the consumer. Its four features: slopes down, bends in towards O (convex), higher is better, never cross.

Exam answer

  • An indifference curve joins all bundles of two goods that give the consumer the same satisfaction, so she is indifferent among them.
  • A consumer's preferences over all bundles can be shown by a family (group) of indifference curves. This is called her indifference map.
  • Preferences are monotonic (she prefers more to less). So bundles on a higher curve are preferred to bundles on a lower curve: IC4 is better than IC3, IC3 better than IC2, and IC2 better than IC1. The arrow in the diagram shows this.

Features of indifference curves:

  1. Slope downward from left to right: to get more bananas while staying equally happy, she must give up some mangoes.
  2. Convex to the origin: because of the law of diminishing marginal rate of substitution (MRS). She gives up fewer and fewer mangoes for each extra banana.
  3. A higher curve gives a higher level of satisfaction.
  4. Two indifference curves never intersect each other.

Micro Ch 1 · Introduction

What are the three central problems of an economy? Explain.4 marks

💡 Remember: What to produce? How to produce? For whom to produce? They all come from scarcity: resources are limited, so every society must choose.

Exam answer

Production, exchange and consumption are the basic economic activities. Every society faces scarcity of resources, and scarcity creates the problem of choice. Because scarce resources have competing uses, every society must decide how to use them. These decisions are the three central problems:

1. What is produced, and in what quantities? How much of each good and service to produce. For example:

  • more food, clothing and housing, or more luxury goods?
  • more agricultural goods, or more industrial goods and services?
  • more resources for education and health, or for the military?
  • more consumption goods today, or more investment goods (like machines) that raise production tomorrow?

2. How are these goods produced? How much of which resources to use for each good. For example, more labour or more machines? Which of the available technologies to use?

3. For whom are these goods produced? How the output is shared among people: who gets more and who gets less. For example, should everyone be given a minimum amount of consumption? Should elementary education and basic health services be free for all?

Conclusion: the allocation of scarce resources and the distribution of the final goods and services are the central problems of any economy.

Briefly explain the production possibility frontier.4 marks

💡 Remember: on the PPF = all resources fully used. Inside = resources idle or wasted. Outside = impossible with today's resources. More of one good always means less of the other (opportunity cost).

Exam answer

The production possibility frontier (PPF) is the curve showing the combinations of two goods that an economy can produce when its limited resources are fully used, with the given technology. It shows the maximum amount of one good that can be produced for any given amount of the other.

  • All the combinations that can be produced from the given resources and technology make up the production possibility set. The PPF is its graph.
  • Example: an economy can produce corn and cotton. If it uses all its resources for corn, it gets at most 4 units of corn. If it uses all of them for cotton, it gets at most 10 units of cotton. It can also produce in-between combinations, like 2 corn and 7 cotton.
  • A point inside the PPF means some resources are unemployed or used wastefully.
  • Using more resources for corn leaves fewer for cotton. The amount of the other good given up to get one more unit of a good is its opportunity cost.
  • The economy has to choose one of these possibilities. This choice is itself one of the central problems of an economy.

How does the Market economy work? Explain.4 marks

💡 Remember: in a market economy, price is the signal. Buyers want more → price rises → producers make more. No government is needed to plan this.

Exam answer

In a market economy, all economic activities are organised through the market, by buyers and sellers, and not by the government.

  1. Meaning of market: a market is an institution that allows individuals to interact freely. It is a set of arrangements where people can freely exchange their goods, services or products with each other.
  2. Not just a place: buyers and sellers need not meet in one place. They can trade in a village chowk, a city supermarket, or over the telephone or the internet.
  3. Prices: every good and service has a price, agreed between buyers and sellers, at which it is exchanged. The price shows, on average, how much society values that good.
  4. Price signals: if buyers want more of a good, its price rises. This tells producers that society wants more of it, so they produce more. If buyers want less, the price falls and producers make less. In this way, prices carry information to everyone in the market.
  5. Coordination: through these price signals, the activities of millions of separate buyers and sellers are coordinated. This is how the market solves the central problems of what, how much and how to produce.

In reality, all economies are mixed economies: the market does most of the work, but the government takes some important decisions. The USA is close to a market economy, with a very small role for the government.

Discuss how the Family Farm, Weaver and Teacher can use their resources to fulfill their needs in a simple economy.4 marks

💡 Remember: each person has only a few resources. They produce something with them, then exchange it (or the money earned) for everything else they need.

Exam answer

People in a society need many goods and services, such as food, clothing, housing, transport, and the services of teachers and doctors. No one has everything she needs. Each person has only a few resources. She uses them to produce something, and exchanges part of it for the other things she needs.

  • Scarcity and choice: no one has unlimited resources compared to their needs. So each person must choose: more of one thing means giving up some of another. For example, a bigger house may mean giving up some farmland. Better education for the children may mean giving up some luxuries.
  • Society as a whole: what is produced must match what people want. For example, the total corn produced must match the corn people want to eat. If too much is grown, some resources should be moved to producing other goods.
  • So even a simple economy faces two basic problems: allocating its limited resources among different goods, and distributing the goods produced among its people.

Write about the role of the Government (State) and Household sectors in both developed and developing countries.4 marks

💡 Remember: Government: laws, justice, taxes, public services (and sometimes production). Households: consume, work, save, pay taxes, and earn wages, salaries, profit, rent and interest.

Exam answer

Role of the Government (State): in both developed and developing countries, the State exists alongside the private sector. Its roles are:

  1. Framing laws and enforcing them
  2. Delivering justice
  3. Imposing and collecting taxes
  4. Spending on public infrastructure, and running schools, colleges and health services
  5. In many cases, undertaking production itself

These economic functions must be counted when describing a country's economy. For convenience, the State is called the "Government".

Role of Households:

  1. A household is a single person who takes decisions about her own consumption, or a group of people whose consumption decisions are taken jointly.
  2. Households consume goods. The market where firms sell their products could not work without the demand coming from households.
  3. Households save and pay taxes.
  4. Household members work and earn incomes: wages from working in firms, salaries from government jobs, and profits as owners of firms. They can also earn rent by leasing land and interest by lending capital.

Write about the emergence of Macroeconomics.4 marks

💡 Remember: classical economists said "everyone who wants work will find it". Then the Great Depression (1929) proved them wrong, and Keynes explained why in his book of 1936. That is how macroeconomics was born.

Exam answer

  1. Birth of the subject: macroeconomics became a separate branch of economics after the British economist John Maynard Keynes published The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money in 1936.
  2. Classical tradition: before Keynes, economists believed that all labourers who are ready to work will find jobs, and all factories will work at full capacity.
  3. The Great Depression of 1929: in 1929 and the years after it, output and employment fell hugely in Europe and North America, and other countries were affected too. Demand for goods was low, many factories lay idle, and workers lost their jobs.
  4. Size of the fall in the USA: from 1929 to 1933, unemployment rose from 3% to 25%, and total output fell by about 33%.
  5. Need for a new theory: these events made economists think about the economy in a new way. They had to explain how unemployment could last for a long time. Keynes's book tried to do this.
  6. Keynes's new approach: unlike earlier economists, he studied the working of the economy as a whole, and how its different sectors depend on each other. With this, macroeconomics was born.
