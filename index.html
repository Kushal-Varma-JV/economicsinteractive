Micro Ch 3 · Production and Costs

Explain the various short run costs.6 marks

💡 Remember: TC = TFC + TVC. Divide each one by output (q) to get the averages. SMC always passes through the lowest points of AVC and SAC, coming up from below.

Exam answer

In the short run, some inputs are fixed (for example, the factory building) and some can be changed (for example, workers and raw materials). So there are seven short run costs:

CostWhat it meansFormulaShape of curve
1. Total Fixed Cost (TFC)Cost of the fixed inputs, like rent. It stays the same even when nothing is produced.–Flat line
2. Total Variable Cost (TVC)Cost of the variable inputs, like wages. It is zero when nothing is produced and rises with output.–Starts at 0 and rises
3. Total Cost (TC)Fixed and variable cost added togetherTFC + TVCSame shape as TVC, lifted up by TFC
4. Average Fixed Cost (AFC)Fixed cost per unitTFC ÷ qKeeps falling (a rectangular hyperbola)
5. Average Variable Cost (AVC)Variable cost per unitTVC ÷ qU-shaped
6. Short run Average Cost (SAC)Total cost per unitTC ÷ q (= AVC + AFC)U-shaped, above AVC
7. Short run Marginal Cost (SMC)Extra cost of making one more unitΔTC ÷ ΔqU-shaped

Why does SMC pass through the lowest points?

Think of your average marks. If your next test score is below your average, your average goes down. If it is above your average, your average goes up.

  • While SMC is below AVC, AVC keeps falling.
  • Once SMC goes above AVC, AVC starts rising.
  • So SMC must cross AVC exactly at AVC's lowest point, P. The same thing happens with SAC at its lowest point, S.

Why S is to the right of P: SAC = AVC + AFC, and AFC keeps falling. So SAC keeps falling for a little longer than AVC does.

Explain the shapes of Total Product (TP), Average Product (AP) and Marginal Product (MP) curves.6 marks

💡 Remember: TP climbs and then flattens. MP and AP are both hills (∩). MP is the steeper hill, and it cuts AP at the top of AP's hill, from above.

Exam answer

Meanings (labour changes, all other inputs stay fixed):

  • Total Product (TP): the total output from a given number of workers.
  • Average Product (AP): output per worker. AP = TP ÷ L.
  • Marginal Product (MP): the extra output from one more worker. MP = TP now − TP before.

Why the curves have these shapes (law of variable proportions): when one input is increased and the others stay fixed, its MP first rises and then falls.

Example: a farmer has 4 hectares of land. With one worker there is too much land for one person, so each new worker adds more and more output. After a point the land gets crowded, and each new worker adds less.

  • TP curve: slopes upward. It rises fast at first, then more slowly.
  • MP curve: inverse U-shaped. It rises, reaches a peak, then falls.
  • AP curve: also inverse U-shaped, but flatter. It stays below MP while it is rising.
  • MP and AP: while MP is above AP, AP rises. Once MP falls below AP, AP falls (just like average marks). So MP cuts AP from above at AP's highest point (P).

💡 Remember: in perfect competition every unit sells at the same price. So TR goes up by Rs 30 each time, and MR = AR = Price = Rs 30.

Exam answer

  • Total Revenue (TR): the money earned from selling all the units. TR = Price × Quantity.
  • Marginal Revenue (MR): the extra money from selling one more unit. MR = TR now − TR before.
  • Average Revenue (AR): the money earned per unit sold. AR = TR ÷ Quantity.

Micro Ch 5 · Market Equilibrium

Draw a diagram for the following table and identify the equilibrium point, equilibrium price, equilibrium quantity, excess demand and excess supply in the diagram.Price (P): 10, 20, 30, 40, 50
Quantity demanded (QD): 25, 20, 15, 10, 5
Quantity supplied (QS): 5, 10, 15, 20, 255 marks

💡 Remember: equilibrium is where demand = supply. Below that price, people want more than is supplied (excess demand). Above it, more is supplied than people want (excess supply).

Exam answer

  • Equilibrium point: E, where the demand curve DD and the supply curve SS cross.
  • Equilibrium price: Rs 30, because here QD = QS = 15.
  • Equilibrium quantity: 15 units.
  • Excess demand (prices below Rs 30): at Rs 20, 20 − 10 = 10 units. At Rs 10, 25 − 5 = 20 units.
  • Excess supply (prices above Rs 30): at Rs 40, 20 − 10 = 10 units. At Rs 50, 25 − 5 = 20 units.

What the words mean:

  • Market equilibrium: the situation where market demand equals market supply.
  • Equilibrium price: the price at which demand equals supply.
  • Equilibrium quantity: the quantity bought and sold at that price.
  • Excess demand: demand is more than supply.
  • Excess supply: supply is more than demand.

Suppose the demand and supply curves of salt are given by qD = 100 − P and qS = 70 + 2Pa) Find the equilibrium price and quantity
b) Suppose the government has imposed a tax of Rs.3 per unit of sale of salt, how does it affect the equilibrium price and quantity?6 marks

💡 Remember: at equilibrium, demand = supply. Set the two equations equal and solve for P. For a tax of Rs t, sellers keep only P − t from each unit, so write (P − t) in place of P in the supply equation.

a) Equilibrium price and quantity

Set demand equal to supply:
100 − P = 70 + 2P
100 − 70 = 2P + P
30 = 3P
P = 10

Put P = 10 into either equation:
qD = 100 − 10 = 90
qS = 70 + 2(10) = 90
Equilibrium quantity = 90

b) After a tax of Rs 3 per unit

With the tax, sellers keep only (P − 3) from each unit. So the supply equation becomes qS = 70 + 2(P − 3).

100 − P = 70 + 2(P − 3)
100 − P = 70 + 2P − 6
100 − P = 64 + 2P
36 = 3P
P = 12

qD = 100 − 12 = 88
qS = 70 + 2(12 − 3) = 70 + 18 = 88
New equilibrium quantity = 88

The tax raises the equilibrium price (10 → 12) and lowers the equilibrium quantity (90 → 88).

Micro Ch 2 · Theory of Consumer Behaviour

A consumer wants to consume two goods, the price of good X1 is Rs. 15 and the price of good X2 is Rs. 25. The consumer's income is Rs. 300. Answer the following.a) How many X1 goods a consumer can consume if the entire income is spent on that good?
b) How many X2 goods a consumer can consume if the entire income is spent on that good?
c) Is the slope of budget line downward or upward?
d) Are the bundles on the budget line equal to the consumer income or not?
e) If consumer wants to have more of X1 good, X2 good has to be given up. Is it true?5 marks

💡 Remember: income ÷ price = the most you can buy of that good. The budget line always slopes down, every bundle on it costs the whole income, and more of one good means less of the other.

Exam answer

a) 300 ÷ 15 = 20 units of X1.

b) 300 ÷ 25 = 12 units of X2.

c) The slope of the budget line is downward.

d) Yes. Every bundle on the budget line costs exactly the consumer's income of Rs 300. Check: 10 units of X1 and 6 units of X2 cost (10 × 15) + (6 × 25) = 150 + 150 = 300.

e) True. The income is fixed. Spending more on X1 leaves less money for X2, so some X2 has to be given up.

Diagram

Why it slopes down: the budget line is 15 X1 + 25 X2 = 300. Its slope is −15/25 = −0.6. So each extra unit of X1 costs 0.6 units of X2. That is why 5 more X1 cost 3 X2.

Explain the law of Diminishing Marginal Utility with the help of table and diagram.6 marks

💡 Remember: the first glass of water on a hot day feels great. The fifth one, not so much. Each extra unit gives less satisfaction than the one before.

Exam answer

The law: the marginal utility from each additional unit of a commodity falls as more of it is consumed, while the consumption of other commodities stays the same.

Why it happens: once the consumer has some of the good, her desire to have still more of it becomes weaker.

Meanings:

  • Total utility (TU): the total satisfaction from consuming a given amount of a good.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the extra satisfaction from one more unit. MU = TU now − TU before.

Explain the optimal choice of consumer with the help of diagram.6 marks

💡 Remember: best choice = the point where the budget line just touches the highest indifference curve it can reach. At that point, MRS = price ratio.

Exam answer

  1. The budget line shows the bundles the consumer can afford with her income.
  2. An indifference curve (IC) shows bundles she likes equally. A higher IC means more satisfaction.
  3. She is rational, so she wants to reach the highest IC she can afford.
  4. Her best choice is point E, where the budget line just touches (is tangent to) IC2. She buys x1* bananas and x2* mangoes.
  5. Why not other points? Bundles on IC3 cost more than her income. Every other point on the budget line lies on a lower curve, like IC1.
  6. Why on the line and not below it? Below the line she has money left over. She could buy more of a good and be better off.
  7. Condition at E: MRS = p1/p2. MRS is the rate at which she is willing to swap mangoes for bananas. p1/p2 is the rate at which the market lets her swap.

Why MRS must equal p1/p2: suppose both fruits cost the same, but she would happily give up 2 mangoes for 1 extra banana (MRS = 2). The market asks for only 1 mango. So she can buy the extra banana and still have more of both goods than she was willing to settle for. That point cannot be her best choice. Only where the two rates are equal is there no such gain left.

Illustrate the movement along the demand curve and shift in demand curve with the help of two diagrams.6 marks

💡 Remember: the good's own price changes → move along the same curve. Anything else changes (income, prices of related goods, tastes) → the whole curve shifts.

Exam answer

The amount of a good a consumer buys depends on its own price, the prices of other goods, her income, and her tastes. The demand curve is drawn keeping everything except the good's own price fixed.

1. Movement along the demand curve: happens when only the price of the good changes. The consumer moves to another point on the same curve.

  • Price falls → she buys more (moves down the curve). This is called extension of demand.
  • Price rises → she buys less (moves up the curve). This is called contraction of demand.

2. Shift in the demand curve: happens when something other than the good's own price changes. The whole curve moves.

  • Shifts right (more is bought at every price): income rises for a normal good, the price of a substitute rises, the price of a complement falls, or tastes change in favour of the good (for example, ice-cream in summer).
  • Shifts left (less is bought at every price): income rises for an inferior good, the price of a complement rises, or tastes turn against the good (for example, news that cold drinks harm health).
